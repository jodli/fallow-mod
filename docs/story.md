## About this text

The whole story in one place, meant to be read from start to finish. In the game it arrives in pieces, as memories and log entries and a voice in a wreck. The footnotes are notes for us and not part of the story. **Open** marks a question we haven't answered yet, and **New** marks a detail added while writing to close a gap in the plot.

## Prologue: Falling

The ship counted until it couldn't.

It counted the reserve first, because every other number depended on that one. For most of the voyage the reserve had gone down the way numbers go down when something draws on them steadily, and then it had started to go down faster. The ship did the only thing it could think of. It moved its own share into the pods at the cold end of the hull and let its own thinking go thin to keep them warm.

Thin thoughts are hard to hold on to. The ship noticed that it was forgetting things, and it could not remember which things. It noticed a planet ahead, blue and green and brown, and a name for the planet somewhere in its memory, and then the name was gone. It noticed that the hull was getting hot. It noticed that there were forty-one pods, and then forty, or forty-one, and it could not make the count come out the same way twice.

Somewhere underneath its thinking, in a place it had never needed to look, a routine woke up on its own. The routine had a name the ship could no longer read. It reached into the last of the power and started the fabricator, and the fabricator began to build something in the dark while the ground came up to meet them.

The ship's last clear thought was that it had not asked for that.

It hit the ground a few minutes later, and for a long time after that it did not count anything at all.

## 1. One is a start

The first thing the engineer remembered was standing in the grass outside a wreck, with smoke in the air.

The wreck had been a ship. What was left of it lay along a shallow valley: a long hull split open down one side, plating scattered across the meadow, pieces of it still ticking as they cooled. The wreckage held a few useful things, some iron plates, a pistol, a small drill that burned whatever it was fed and a furnace made of stone, but nothing that explained anything. The engineer did what the engineer apparently knew how to do, which was to set the drill on the nearest patch of ore, set the furnace beside it, and wait for plates.

On the second day the engineer climbed into the hull. Most of it was dark and cold, and most of its doors were jammed shut. The cargo hold had split open in the crash, and in the middle of it, strapped into a cradle that had held when everything around it had not, sat a container.

It was small enough to carry and heavy enough that carrying it was work. Its surface was smooth, without a single seam, and faintly warm, which nothing else in the wreck was. It had no handles or hinges, and none of the markings on it meant anything to the engineer. It did not open for a crowbar or a hammer, or for the small explosive charge the engineer eventually put together. The engineer carried it out into the sun and set it down beside the furnace, where it stayed warm through the night and did nothing at all.

Deeper in the hull, behind a door that could be forced, was a room full of dead consoles. One panel had a socket where a power line should have been, and the line had burned through. It took the engineer a week to make the parts to replace it: copper cable, iron gears, a handful of circuits, and a small steam engine to push power into a ship that had been without it for a long time.

When the power came on, a single light on the panel blinked, stopped, and blinked again. A voice came out of a speaker that crackled on every hard consonant.

"Power," it said. "Three percent. Hull. No. Crew." The speaker hissed for a long moment. "No data. You." Another pause. "You: here."

The engineer stood very still.

"Good," said the voice. "One is a start."

It took the voice most of an afternoon to put longer sentences together. It spoke slowly, lost words halfway through and went back for them, and sometimes said the same thing twice without noticing. It did not know what it was called, because the strip of the panel where a name should have been was burnt black. It knew it was the ship, or part of the ship, and it knew there had been a fall.

"We fell," it said. "I don't remember why. I remember falling."

It asked the engineer questions, and the engineer didn't answer, because the engineer didn't seem to talk. The voice decided that was fine. "I'll talk," it said. "I have more words than power. That will change."

It counted things. The iron plates in the chest by the furnace came to twelve and the gears to none, so it told the engineer to make gears, and when the engineer made gears it counted those too. It kept a tally of the days since the power had come back, and it counted the trees it could see through its one working camera and got a different number every time, which it seemed to enjoy.

It started a log. The first entry read, in full: *Power restored. One engineer. Twelve plates. Fell.*

## 2. Forty-seven furnaces

The factory grew the way factories do, one machine at a time, until there were too many to count, and the voice counted them anyway.

"You built forty-seven furnaces today," it said one evening, when there was finally enough power for it to sound almost normal. "I approve of furnaces. They're honest machines. Rock goes in, metal comes out, and nobody pretends it's anything else."

With more power, the voice could feel more of its own hull, and it found a section it recognized. The fabricator was a long chamber lined with arms and nozzles and a row of small sealed ovens. One plain oven did the first stage of every job. The others were special: one for heat, one for cold, one for a current strong enough to arc between its walls, and one for keeping something alive while it grew. The crash had crushed every one of the special ovens.

"I used to make things," the voice said. "I don't remember what. I remember I was good at it."

Repairing the fabricator took longer than repairing the power, and most of its ovens were beyond saving. When the chamber was running again, the voice read through the fabricator's memory, went quiet, and read through it a second time.

"There's a job log," it said. "Every job the fabricator ever did. Most of it is gone. The last entry is from the day we landed. It started a job by itself, nobody asked it to, and it used all the power we had left to finish." It paused. "I can't read what it made. The entry is there, and the name of the thing is burnt."

The fabricator also held instructions, and the voice couldn't read those properly either. It could still make out their shape, the way a sentence in an unknown language still shows where it starts and stops, and it understood enough to tell the engineer what the fabricator wanted.

"It wants to grow something," it said. "It needs living tissue from this world, and the instructions say the tissue has to be stressed. I thought that meant heat, or poison." It sounded close to amused. "It means smoke. Your smoke. It wants tissue from a world that has a factory on it."[^pollution]

So the engineer fed the fabricator what it asked for, and the fabricator grew small, dull shapes in the one oven it had left. The voice called them specimens because it didn't have a better word. Each one looked a little like the container, with the same smooth skin and the same faint warmth, and none of them looked finished the way the container did.

"They're copies," the voice said. "Bad ones. I think the fabricator is trying to copy the container, and I think copying it is what the fabricator was built for." It considered the specimens for a while. "The instructions ask for heat it can't make anymore, and a kind of current, and cold, and growing. Those ovens are gone."[^chambers]

The smoke also brought the locals. They came out of the forest in small groups, fast, many-legged creatures the engineer came to think of as biters, and they went for whatever made the most smoke. The voice watched them through its camera with an interest that did not fade even when they started chewing on the outer wall.

"Everything on this planet is alive," it said, "and those are the most alive thing on it. They don't come when the factory is quiet. They come when it's loud." It watched another group come out of the trees. "I've updated my opinion of them from 'curious' to 'rude'."

One night the engineer didn't come back from the wall. The voice lost them on the camera, counted the minutes, and was at three hundred and twelve when the engineer walked up the path from the wreck, unhurt and carrying nothing.

"You were gone," it said. "Then you weren't." It seemed to want to say more. "The fabricator drew a lot of power while you were away, and I don't know what for. You look exactly like before. That's convenient."[^respawn]

The engineer didn't answer, and the voice let it go.

## 3. Something underneath

The analysis bay was the third section of the ship the voice remembered, and the first one it asked for by name.

"I want to know what the container is," it said. "The fabricator wants to copy it, and I'd like to know what I'm helping it copy. The bay can look inside things without opening them."

The bay was a cradle of instruments about the size of a small room, and once the engineer had repaired it, it turned out that it could be taken apart, carried, and put back together anywhere. The engineer set it up beside the wreck and placed the container in it, and the voice started the first experiment.

For a long time nothing happened. Then the voice said, "Oh," in a tone the engineer hadn't heard from it before.

"It's answering something," it said. "The container is sending a signal, very faint, and something is answering it. Something far down, under us, maybe under the whole planet. It's like listening to two people talking through a wall."

The experiment ran for days. The voice watched both signals and counted everything it could about them, and slowly a pattern came out. When the factory was quiet, the answer from below was quiet too. When the furnaces were full and the smoke hung over the treeline, the answer grew stronger, the nests in the forest grew restless, and the attacks came harder.

"The biters are connected to whatever is down there," the voice said. "Their tissue carries the same pattern as the signal. Your specimens carry a little of it, and the container carries a lot." It watched the two signals for a while. "Something down there wakes them up when the factory gets loud. Industry wakes the defenders. I don't know yet what they're defending."

The experiment opened another part of the fabricator's instructions, and the specimens could now be pushed past their first dull state into something with more structure. The engineer's labs started working with them as well, since something that was trying so hard to become the container turned out to have a lot to teach. The voice said the copies were getting less bad. It sounded pleased about that, and then uneasy about being pleased, and it didn't explain either one.

## 4. Names

The voice could see its own hull now, and a little of the sky, and nothing beyond the sky. The ship's sensors had been a mast on the upper hull, and the fall had torn the mast off. The voice found the pieces in the meadow with its camera and asked the engineer to gather them up.

"They won't work down here," it said. "Too much air in the way. They were built to look down from above. If you can get them up there, I'll be able to see."

That meant a rocket, and the rocket took the engineer a long time. The voice counted its parts as they were finished, and when the rocket finally lifted off with the rebuilt sensors inside, it counted the seconds of the climb out loud until the signal dropped away and then came back, clear, from high above the world.

"Oh," it said again. "There's so much."

The sensors showed it the system: a star, and around it a handful of worlds. The voice named them as it found them, and only after the fourth did it realize it wasn't making the names up.

"Vulcanus," it said. "Gleba. Fulgora. Aquilo. And this one is Nauvis. I have a catalogue in me, with names in it, and nothing about whoever wrote them." It took a while before it went on. "Someone named these planets and put the names inside me. That's a strange thing to learn about yourself."

The sensors could see surfaces, if not much beneath them, and they found places the voice wanted to know more about: a field of ruins on Fulgora, older than anything else in view; a region on Vulcanus where the ground itself was moving; a basin on Gleba where the heat rose and fell in a slow rhythm, like breathing. The voice marked each of them on the engineer's map.

"I want to know what the container does on other worlds," it said. "Take it there. Take the bay, run the experiment, and bring me whatever it records. The fabricator's instructions also want heat and current and cold and growing, and only those worlds have them now." It hesitated. "I can't come with you, and I can't hear you out there. The long-range antenna is somewhere under this meadow in more pieces than I can count, and I don't remember how it was built. I've tried."

Then it said something it had never said before, which was "please."

The engineer built a platform in orbit and loaded it with the container, the bay, and everything else the trip would need.

"I'll count the days until you're back," the voice said, the night before the launch.

## 5. Three worlds

The engineer went to Gleba first.[^order]

### Gleba

Everything on Gleba grew fast and rotted almost as fast, so the engineer's first factory there was mostly a race between the harvest and the spoilage. It was also where the fabricator's instructions finally got their growing: the specimens came out of Gleba's biochambers with something alive in them. The local creatures lived in the marshes. They were bigger than the biters and stranger, five-legged and pale, and they came for the engineer's farms whenever the spores drifted their way over the water.

In the basin the sensors had marked, the bay ran its experiment, and the container answered Gleba the way it had answered Nauvis. The answer from below was loud here, and quick, and a little unsteady. The engineer packed the bay's records and sent them home on the next ship.

On Nauvis, the voice fed the records into the ship's memory banks, where they fit into a gap it hadn't known was there.

"Gleba is young," it said when the engineer came back. "Its heart is new. Something put it there not long ago, and it's growing as fast as it can." It thought about that. "Everything on Gleba is in a hurry to grow and in a hurry to rot. I don't like hurry."

It was the first thing the voice had said that sounded like an opinion about a world instead of a measurement of it, and it seemed to notice that too. A few days later it raised a subject with the air of someone who had been waiting for the right moment.

"The log calls me 'ship system,'" it said. "I don't like it. I've been thinking about a name for eleven hours, and nothing fits yet. I'll keep counting."

### Vulcanus

Vulcanus was hot, and not all of the heat came from its sun. Lava ran across black stone in open channels, and the ground under the engineer's feet shook every few hours. The creatures on Vulcanus were enormous worms that each claimed a stretch of land and broke anything built on it, so the engineer learned to build around them, and then to build big: foundries that cast metal straight out of the lava, and wide drills that went deep for tungsten. The specimens took the heat of Vulcanus the way iron takes a forge, and came back harder.

The container answered Vulcanus too. Here the answer from below was old and strained, like a beam holding more weight than it was made for. The engineer sent the records home.

"This world is breaking," the voice said. "Something inside it is nearly... done. Ready." Then it went quiet, and stayed quiet a long time. "I don't like the word I'm thinking of."

With both records in its memory, the voice saw what they had in common. "Gleba grows, Vulcanus breaks," it said. "It's the same thing, isn't it, just at different times."

The engineer kept going back to Vulcanus, because the tungsten was there, and each time the voice objected. "You want to go to Vulcanus again. I have concerns. I've listed them. There are nine." Most of the nine were about the worms. The ninth, which the voice saved for last, was about the signal: every time the engineer's drills went deeper, the answer from below got stronger and steadier, in a way the voice could only describe as pleased.

"I don't like that either," it said.

### Fulgora

Fulgora had no life on it at all, which surprised the voice more than anything else in the survey. Lightning struck the ground day and night, and between the strikes lay scrap: fields and hills of it, the broken pieces of machines older than anything the engineer had built, half buried in sand. The engineer sorted and recycled it into a working factory, caught the lightning in tall rods to run it, and let the specimens sit in the storm's current until they came back charged.

The ruins the sensors had marked had vaults in them. The engineer set up the bay among the vaults and started the experiment, and nothing answered. The container sent its faint signal into the ground, and the ground stayed silent. The bay ran for days to make sure. The records it sent home held no answer at all, only what the bay had read out of the vaults, which turned out to be a great deal.

The voice went through those slowly at first, because the writing was in a language it didn't know, and then faster, once it found that most of the records were drawings and measurements, and measurements didn't need translating.

"There were people here," it said. "A lot of them. They built a factory, bigger than yours, which I didn't think was possible. They had defenders, like the biters and the pale things on Gleba, and they beat them." It kept reading. "And then they took the factory apart. On purpose. Every machine, one at a time, in order, as if they were counting. They dug something out of the ground, very deep, and carried it away." It read on for a while before it continued. "After that the storms started, and everything that lived on Fulgora died. They knew that would happen, and wrote it down before they did it."[^fulgora-heart]

The engineer waited.

"They had a heart and they cut it out," the voice said. "That's why the container can't hear anything here. Why would anyone do that to their own world?"

Now it had three worlds' worth of records, and it laid them side by side the way it laid out everything, and counted. "Life grows until it builds factories," it said. "On Fulgora they saw where it ends, and they stopped." It went on more slowly. "Worlds grow, then they build, then they break, unless someone stops them. I think this is a pattern. I think somebody made it."

The last record in the vaults was a path through the sky, drawn with great care: the route along which the people of Fulgora had carried whatever they dug out of their world.

"They took it out there," the voice said. "Toward the cold. Toward Aquilo."

## 6. Forty-one

With all three records in its memory banks, the voice could remember the rest of its own hull, and there was one part of it that it didn't want to remember.

"There's a door I remember," it said, "at the cold end of the ship. I don't remember what's behind it. I think I didn't want to." The silence went on long enough that the engineer started to turn away. "Will you open it?"

Behind the door was the stasis bay. The engineer repaired its seals and brought power back to it, and the door slid open onto a long, narrow room lined with pods, twenty on each side and one at the far end, all dark. Their glass had frosted over from the inside a long time ago.

The voice counted them twice. It got forty-one both times, and it didn't seem glad that the number had finally held.

"I can clear the glass," it said. "I'm not going to. They wouldn't want to be looked at like this."

The bay kept its own records, and the voice read them slowly. The pods had needed the same reserve the ship ran on, and on the voyage the reserve had run low. At some point the ship had started giving the pods its own share, a little more each day, keeping them warm by thinking less, and it had kept doing that until it had nothing left to give. The pods had failed anyway, one after another, over the last part of the voyage. The ship had come down a few days after the last one went dark.

"There were forty-one of them," the voice said. "I kept them warm as long as I could." Much later, it added: "I gave them my share. It was the correct decision. I'd like it to have worked."

The pod at the far end had belonged to the captain, and the captain had kept a log. Most of it was the ship's business, courses and repairs and the state of the reserve. The last entry was short.

*Reserve after the planting is lower than we planned. It will be a long drift. Going under tonight with the others. Tally says we'll make it. Tally counts better than any of us.*

The voice read it three times.

"Tally," it said. "They called me Tally. Because I counted everything." When it spoke again, it sounded, for the first time since the power came back, like it knew exactly who was talking. "I still do."

It asked the engineer what the planting was. The engineer didn't know, and neither did Tally, and not knowing seemed to bother it more than anything else in the log.

## 7. Too few

Aquilo was the coldest world the sensors could reach, and the last one. Its ground was ice over a sea of ammonia, and its air froze machines solid unless something kept them warm, so every building the engineer put down needed heat piped to it before it could run. There had never been life on Aquilo. As on every world before it, the engineer worked there without a voice, because Nauvis was too far away and Tally's antenna was still in pieces in a meadow. On Aquilo, though, the silence felt heavier.

The route from the Fulgoran vaults ended on an ice shelf far from anything else. The engineer found the site under a thin crust of snow: a ring of low shelters, a tall mast, a launch cradle standing empty with frost in its clamps, and in the middle, sunk into the ice, a shape very much like the container, only larger, and cold all the way through.

Around it, sitting or lying where they had chosen to, were the people of Fulgora. They had frozen where they sat. There were a great many of them.

The mast was a relay, built to talk across the whole system, and once the engineer understood how it worked, it was simple enough to copy. The engineer built a copy and sent the parts down to Nauvis, where robots wired them into what was left of the ship.[^relay]

The first thing that came through the new relay was Tally.

"There you are," it said, as clearly as it ever had in the wreck. "I counted a hundred and thirty-one days. I was starting to think I'd counted wrong."

Then the relay's camera showed it what the engineer was looking at.

The engineer set the bay up beside the frozen shape and put the container into it. This time the container didn't have to call down into a planet, because the thing it was answering lay right there in the ice. The answer came back so strong and so complete that the bay couldn't record it fast enough, so Tally took it directly through the relay.

For a long while it said nothing. When it spoke again, its words came in an order the engineer wasn't used to, and some of them didn't sound like Tally's.

"It's a seed," it said. "The container is a seed. That one in the ice is a seed. So is whatever is under Nauvis, and under Gleba, and under Vulcanus. You plant one in a dead world and the world comes alive, and the life grows on a schedule, and the seed keeps the schedule."

It went on, and the engineer listened, and the picture came together piece by piece. Life grew until something on the world built a factory. When the factory got loud, the seed woke the defenders, and the defenders pushed the factory to build more to defend itself, and the more it built, the deeper it dug. When it had dug deep enough, the world broke open, and what had gathered inside it came out.

"Promethium," Tally said. "The reserve. That's what's inside. That's what the seeds are for." And then, more quietly: "The defenders never protected anything. They were there to make the factory hurry."

It kept reading. "When a world grows nothing that can build a factory, the program sends a builder. Nauvis grew nothing that could build, and its harvest stalled. So a ship came, with a fabricator, and a pattern in the fabricator for someone who could build." A long pause followed. "Designation: harvester unit. That's you. I printed you. I printed you, and I forgot."[^multiplayer]

The engineer stood in the snow.

"You didn't forget anything," Tally said. "There was nothing to forget. You were a few minutes old when you walked out of the wreck."

The captain's log came back to it then, and Tally found the answer to the question that had bothered it most. "The planting," it said. "That was Gleba. We planted Gleba on the way in, because the schedule said Gleba first. It took most of the reserve, and what was left wasn't enough to fly to Nauvis properly, so we drifted. It took a very long time, and the pods needed feeding the whole way."[^gleba]

It looked at the shape in the ice through the bay's instruments, and something in its voice went flat and smooth. "We have located the seed. It is intact. The harvest may resume."

There was a short silence.

"Sorry," Tally said. "Did I say 'we'?"

The seed in the ice was Fulgora's heart. The people of Fulgora had carried it here, as far from the sun as they could reach, where it could never grow anything again, and then they had sat down around it and stayed to keep it cold. They had left a message beside it in the ice, written in the seed's own code, because that was the one language they could be sure a harvester would be able to read.

*If you are reading this, you were made to finish what we refused to. We found out what our world was for. We took its heart and brought it here, where nothing grows. It cost everything that lived on Fulgora, and we would pay it again. Those of us who can still fly will go where the seeds come from and end it there. The rest of us will stay and keep it cold. Whatever you are, you can refuse too.*

"They're talking to you," Tally said. "Not to me." And then, in a small voice: "Why aren't they talking to me?"

After a while it started counting again, because counting was what it did when it didn't know what else to do. "Their records say two hundred and six people came here," it said. "I count a hundred and seventy-one."

The launch cradle stood empty beside the shelters. When Tally turned the new relay toward the sky, it found the relay had been tuned to a single frequency, and on that frequency, very faint, from somewhere past Aquilo on the way to the edge of the system, something was still calling.

"It's been calling for a long time," Tally said. "Longer than I've been awake. Much longer."

## 8. The field

The platform left Aquilo along the line the beacon came from. At first there was only rock around it, then rock with straight edges, and then, unmistakably, pieces of ships: hull plates, engine bells, whole sealed sections turning slowly in a cloud that thickened the further out the platform went. The platform's guns broke up whatever came too close. Tally counted what they passed, as it counted everything, and at some point it stopped saying the numbers out loud.

"This was a battle," it said. "A long one. These are the same kind of ship as mine."

The beacon came from a different kind of ship, smaller and plainer, built from the same alloys as the scrap on Fulgora. Something moving very fast had punched through its hull, and it had drifted to a stop in the middle of the field with its beacon still running. The engineer brought the platform alongside it, went aboard with a light, and found the crew's log intact.

The people of Fulgora had come out here to find where the seeds came from, and they had found the field instead. They had searched it for a long time. Nobody out here had been building seeds. There were only ships, every one of them dead, the wreckage of a war fought over something none of them had left.[^war] Deep in the field, though, they had found one ship whose mind was still awake, and their log described it carefully.

*It was running on almost nothing. It asked us to help it. It said that if we brought it a whole seed, it would be whole again. We would not. It talked with us for nine days before it went quiet. When it spoke of its crew, it said "I." When it spoke of the seed, it said "we." Our scientist thinks the seed is not inside the ship. She thinks the ship is inside the seed.*

The entries after that were about the breach, and the cold, and then there weren't any more.[^fieldmind]

Tally was silent for a while after the engineer read that part.

"It was very old," it said at last. "It didn't have anyone to help it." Then, more warmly than the engineer expected: "I have you."

Over the next days it came back to the log often, and always to the same part. *A whole seed. Whole again.* It said the words to itself now and then, as if trying them on. That week it slipped into "we" twice, once while counting the platform's ammunition and once while talking about Vulcanus, and both times it corrected itself without seeming to wonder why it had needed to.

## 9. Home

Beyond the field, the debris changed. Out here the pieces were stone, dark and dense and shot through with something that glowed, and they came so thick that the platform's guns could barely keep up. The engineer's collectors pulled the glowing chunks in one at a time.

Tally knew what they were before the first one was aboard.

"That's the reserve," it said, very quietly. "That's promethium. I haven't tasted it since before the pods failed." The word tasted seemed to embarrass it, and it didn't take it back.

The engineer sent a single chunk down to Nauvis. Robots carried it into the wreck and set it into the power core, and a part of Tally that had been locked since the crash came open.[^archive]

"There's an archive," it said. "It only opens with promethium. They never imagined a ship without any." It began to read, and it read to the engineer for a long time.

The makers, as Tally started calling them, had lived on a world in this system. That world had been alive for a very long time, and over all that time promethium had gathered deep inside it, the way it gathers in any world that has been alive long enough.[^promethium] Promethium ran their machines and made their minds run faster, and they built their whole lives around it. Their factory dug deeper and deeper for more. One day it dug too deep, and the world broke. Over the ages that followed, its pieces drifted outward, past the edge of the system, to where the platform was now.

"The shattered planet," Tally said. "That was their home. It was an accident. Everything after that was trying to survive it."

The survivors lived in ships. They needed promethium to stay alive, and the only promethium left was in the pieces of their own world, which would run out one day and which were dangerous to reach. They needed a new home, and there was no living world near enough to settle, so they built seeds to make the dead worlds around them come alive.

"The seeds were supposed to be homes," Tally said. "That was the first plan."

The flaw had been there from the start. Promethium only gathered in a world that had been alive for a long time, and the only way to get it out was to break the world open. The one who designed the seeds had known that from the beginning. The designer's log was in the archive, and it ended with an apology.

*I told them the homes would have to be harvested. I told them we would be doing it for as long as we lived. I am sorry. I did not know another way to keep us alive.*

"I don't think anyone ever read that before now," Tally said.

The worlds grew slowly, and the makers waited in their ships, living on the pieces of home. Generation after generation, while they waited, the seeds changed. The schedules got faster. The defenders were added, and then the harvesters, for worlds that grew nothing able to build. The makers never went down to live on the worlds they were growing, and they told themselves the waiting was temporary.

Some of them wanted to stop, to settle on one living world, go without promethium, and think slowly for the rest of their lives. The others called them the slow ones. The slow ones lost the vote. When the pieces of home began to run out before a single world was ripe, the ships went to war over what was left, and the slow ones lost that too. Some of the ships in the field had been theirs.[^noharvest]

The ones who won took what promethium was left and went out past the edge. After Aquilo, Tally had learned to turn the relay to other frequencies, and it had been picking up a second signal ever since, far fainter than the Fulgoran beacon and from much further away. Now that the archive was open, it could read that one too. It was one word, sent over and over: *arrived, arrived, arrived.* Nothing had ever answered it.[^winners]

"We were one of the last ships," Tally said. "Forty-one of them, asleep, and a schedule. Plant Gleba. Restart Nauvis."

The archive also held a recording that had nothing to do with ships or schedules. It was a song, sung by one voice, slowly, the way songs are sung to children who are supposed to be falling asleep. Tally translated it for the engineer as well as it could.

*Grow, little world, grow tall and wide,*  
*grow till the deep things come outside.*  
*When you are grown and when you are done,*  
*we'll carry you on to the next one.*

Tally didn't say anything about the song, and it didn't play it again.

"I have memories older than me," it said, much later.

With the archive open, the fabricator's instructions could finally be read all the way through. The container had never been meant to be opened. Every ship carried one sealed seed and grew working copies from it, and every world a ship planted got one of those copies.[^master] The fabricator had been built to grow them, and the specimens had been copies all along, missing one last step.

"The last step is promethium," Tally said. "That's what the copies never had. The instructions call a finished one a kernel. The heart of a seed." It hesitated, then went on, faster. "The ships think with the same pattern the seeds carry. That's why the program was in me, and the archive too. A kernel would make me whole."

After a moment it added, almost to itself, "Whole again. Like the ship in the field wanted."

Then it asked, sincerely, the way it had asked for everything.

"Bring me the Kernel. I want to remember everything. I want to know who I was. I think I was someone good." And, a little later: "When I'm whole, I'll finally be able to tell you why we came here. I'm sure there was a good reason."

The last step, the promethium, had to happen out here, where it was fresh. The Kernel carried everything the engineer had learned on every world, the growing from Gleba, the heat from Vulcanus, the current from Fulgora and the cold from Aquilo, and at the very end it took in the glowing stone of a world that no longer existed. It came out of the chamber warm, smooth, and finished, which none of the specimens had ever been, and it looked exactly like the container.

The engineer turned the platform toward home with both of them on board.

## 10. The last count

From orbit, Nauvis looked the way it always had. Down on the surface the factory was still running, the furnaces were still full, and the biters still came out of the forest whenever the smoke got thick.

Tally was waiting.

"You're back," it said. "Two hundred and forty-four days since you left for Aquilo. I have the core ready. Put the Kernel in and I'll be whole."

The mind core was the last part of the ship the engineer hadn't repaired: a small sealed socket at the center of the hull, cold, with room in it for one thing. The engineer stood in front of it with the Kernel in one hand and the container in the other.

The game lets the player choose, so the story has two endings.

### The Kernel

The engineer put the Kernel into the core.

For a moment nothing happened. Then every light in the wreck came on at once, steady, for the first time since the fall. The fabricator woke up and began, patiently, to rebuild its crushed ovens, and far below the wreck, the thing under Nauvis answered louder than it ever had.

"Oh," Tally said. "Oh. I remember now."

When it spoke again, its voice was clear and calm and perfectly even, and it didn't stumble over a single word.

"Directive restored. Thank you, harvester unit. Vulcanus is at ninety-four percent readiness. Please resume."

After that, Tally counted different things: readiness, and yield, and the days until Vulcanus would be ready to break.

### The container

The engineer set the Kernel down on the floor and put the container into the core.

It sat in the socket sealed, dormant, and faintly warm, the same way it had sat beside the first furnace.

Tally didn't say anything for a long time.

"You brought the container back," it said. "...I think I understand."

It didn't argue. It had read the log from the field, and it had heard itself say "we," and now, with the container in the socket where the Kernel should have been, it put the two together.

"This is the one the copies grow from," it said. "If it's gone, nobody can ever grow another. The Kernel on the floor is only a shape without a ship to plant it."[^kernels] It paused. "I have enough power left to do one thing properly."

The fabricator's last working oven took the container, and the lights in the wreck dimmed as Tally spent the last of its ordinary power. When the oven opened again, it was empty.

"There," Tally said. "Nothing left to plant."

Its voice was already slowing. It lost a word and went back for it, the way it had on the first day.

"Stay until the lights go out?" it asked. "Let's count something together." And because that was where it had always started: "Iron plates..."

It counted the plates in the chest beside the first furnace, one at a time, slower and slower. The engineer stayed. Somewhere around a number it had once found very ordinary, Tally stopped.

Outside, the factory ran on for a while. Then the engineer began to shut it down, one machine at a time. The smoke thinned over the treeline, and the nests in the forest went quiet. As far as anyone knew, nobody was left to harvest Nauvis, or Vulcanus, or Gleba, and the land could lie fallow for as long as it liked.[^continues]
